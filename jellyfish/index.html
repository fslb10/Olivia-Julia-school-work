01 / Meet the jellyfish

Small, see-through, and full of surprises.

These three features can help you describe it on your poster.

1

Small and rounded

The swimming body is shaped like a bell or umbrella. A large one is about 25 mm, or 2.5 cm, across.

2

Almost see-through

Its body is translucent. It can look clear with a pale white or greenish colour.

3

A ring of tentacles

Many thin tentacles grow around the bell's edge. Stinging cells help it catch tiny prey.

25
cents

Think: about the width of a Canadian quarter.
A large bell is about 2.5 cm across. This picture is enlarged. Draw a 2.5 cm line on paper to imagine its real size.

02 / A Canadian story

How did it get into our lakes?

Look for the swimming form in calm freshwater lakes, ponds, and sheltered water. It is easiest to notice during warm weather.

Original home

China's Yangtze River region

This is where the species comes from naturally. It is non-native to Canada, which means people helped it reach a place outside its natural home.

Canadian habitat

A hidden home underwater

Its tiny attached form, called a polyp, can live on rocks or submerged wood. You might not see swimming jellyfish even when polyps are present.

Canadian field notes
Choose a province. These are examples of reported locations, not a complete map of its range.

Ontario: Big Gull Lake

Jellyfish blooms were reported in Big Gull Lake, in eastern Ontario, in late summer 2012. Ontario lake stewards have shared information about these sightings. Source [4]

British Columbia: UBC researchers report sightings around Vancouver Island, the Sunshine Coast, and the Lower Mainland. Hotel Lake is one place being studied. Sources [2, 6].

Quebec: Ottawa Riverkeeper reports freshwater jellyfish in Quebec and in lakes within the wider Ottawa River region. Source [3].

How could something so tiny travel so far?

1. A tiny passenger

Small life stages can be carried with water plants or other wet materials.

→

2. A lift to new water

Moving aquatic plants, water, or wet boats and equipment may carry them somewhere new.

→

3. A new population

If conditions are suitable, hidden polyps can survive and produce more jellyfish.

Be precise: Transported water plants are a likely way the species first spread beyond its original home. Scientists do not know the exact journey into every Canadian lake.

03 / The hidden life

Now you see it. Usually, you don’t.

A lake can contain these animals even when no swimming jellyfish are visible. Explore their different forms.

Why this matters: A few hidden polyps can produce many animals. Scientists studying B.C. samples have found genetically identical males, showing that copying can help a population grow.

Does it also reproduce using eggs?

Yes. Where male and female medusae are present, sperm can fertilize eggs. Tiny larvae can then settle and become polyps. Some introduced populations do not have both sexes, so this pathway may not happen.

Sources: [2] UBC[4] FOCA[6] Hotel Lake Advisory[7] Canadian study

04 / Lake impacts

Tiny animal. A shared food supply.

The concern is how it might change a lake's food web. A food web shows how living things are connected through what they eat.

What makes it invasive? This jellyfish comes from outside Canada and can spread to new lakes. It eats tiny animals that native fish also need. Scientists are still studying how much harm it causes here, so say may compete for food rather than claiming fish populations have been destroyed.

Impact 1 · Feeding

Eating zooplankton

Freshwater jellyfish eat tiny drifting animals called zooplankton. Feeding removes some of these animals from the water. A large jellyfish bloom may change zooplankton numbers.

Impact 2 · Possible competition

Sharing food with native animals

Some young native fish eat the same prey. Jellyfish could leave less food for them. Scientists are studying whether this affects fish populations in Canadian lakes.

What do we know?

The species is present in Canada. It eats zooplankton. Its hidden stages can survive when swimming jellyfish are not visible.

What are scientists still asking?

How strongly does it affect native animals? Do the effects differ between lakes? A sighting alone does not prove that fish are dying.

05 / Protect our lakes

Don’t give a jellyfish a lift.

Tiny life stages are hard to spot. Preventing them from reaching another lake is an important way to help.

✦

Clean

With an adult, remove mud, plants, and other material from boats, paddles, footwear, and fishing gear.

↓

Drain

Drain water from boats and equipment before moving to another water body. Do not carry lake water to a different lake.

☀

Dry

Let equipment dry completely before using it somewhere else. Follow local cleaning and inspection instructions.

Never release aquarium contents

Do not empty aquarium water, water-garden plants, or unwanted animals into lakes, streams, or storm drains.

Photograph and report

If you spot one, take a photo from a safe place and note where and when you saw it. Ask an adult to help report it to a local invasive-species group. Leave it in the lake.

EDDMapS reporting · Report in B.C.

Can it sting people?

Its stinging cells catch very small prey. Canadian sources report that its stings generally cannot pierce human skin. Observe wildlife without handling it, and never bring it close to your eyes.

Sources: [1] ISCBC[2] UBC[5] Clean Drain Dry

A good research habit

Do not turn a possibility into a fact.

You can say that the jellyfish may compete with young native fish for food. Do not say it has wiped out Canadian fish populations. The sources do not show that.

Science words, made simple.

Open a word to see its meaning.

Native species

A species that occurs naturally in a place, rather than arriving there through people's actions.

Non-native species

A species moved outside its natural range through people's actions. Not every non-native species becomes invasive.

Invasive species

A non-native species that spreads and causes, or can cause, harm in its new home.

Ecosystem

Living things and the non-living parts of their surroundings, interacting together.

Zooplankton

Small animals that drift in water. Many aquatic animals eat them.

Translucent

Letting light through. You can partly see through it.

Polyp and medusa

A polyp is the tiny attached form. A medusa is the free-swimming jellyfish form.

Bloom

A large number of jellyfish appearing together. It is not a flower.

Competition

When living things need the same limited resource, such as food.

Invertebrate

An animal without a backbone. The freshwater jellyfish is an invertebrate.
